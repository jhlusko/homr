Two Methods, One Corpus

We built a training corpus two independent ways and kept only what both agreed on. Then the same discipline that produced the corpus caught our newest checkpoint regressing — on the one benchmark our own labels could not see.

Engineering write-up · checkpoints 426 → 447 → 448 · every figure paired and reproducible

TL;DR

  • The corpus grew 43% and got stricter at the same time — 3,214 → 4,587 clean pairs, 165 → 234 scores, while the evaluation set shrank to only what two independent construction methods agree on.
  • Checkpoint 447 is a large, real win: +8.56pp on Lieder and +2.32pp on an independent corpus it was never trained for. Both intervals exclude zero.
  • Checkpoint 448 is not. Our own labels say +0.88pp; a paired test says that is noise, and an untouched corpus says −1.23pp. We are not shipping it.
  • The last write-up reported ~+3% for a regression. This one is built so that cannot happen again — the method that would have caught it is described below and is now a committed tool.
  • Human review overturned four automated decisions, every one of them in the same direction: the machine was throwing away good data.
  • The rule that discards 10% of the corpus is measuring the wrong thing. It calls all 417 pairs implied tuplets; only 161 of 546 bars are tuplet-shaped, and 89% of the pairs are grand staves — discarded at 8.4× the rate of single staves by arithmetic this codebase already documents as invalid for them.
  • One known blind spot, stated rather than buried: consensus cannot catch the case where both methods agree and both are wrong. We have a confirmed example.

The number that would have been the headline

Checkpoint 448 scores 92.69% on our held-out Lieder split against 447's 91.81%. Read as a single pair of aggregates — which is how every previous number in this project was read — that is a +0.88pp improvement and the run was a success.

It is not. Here is the same checkpoint judged twice: once by the labels this project built for itself, and once by a corpus this work never touched.

The apparent gain does not survive a paired test. Its confidence interval straddles zero, and on the staves that moved at all, more got worse than got better — the positive aggregate is a handful of staves improving a lot, not the model improving. Meanwhile the independent corpus shows a clear, significant regression in the opposite direction.

Two aggregate percentages cannot express any of that. They were what we had, and they were enough to report a regression as a +3% win once already.

The actual lesson

A benchmark built from labels you rebuilt yourself measures your labels as much as your model. When the training corpus and the evaluation corpus improve together, the honest question is not how much did we gain but would we be able to tell if we had lost.

Apples to apples, three ways

Every checkpoint is now scored on three benchmarks with different exposure to this work, and the comparison is paired: the same staves, per-staff differences, a bootstrap interval over staves, and an explicit better/worse/unchanged split. The tool is training/transformer/compare_checkpoints.py.

The middle row is the one that matters, and it is also the one that makes 447 worth being pleased about: +2.32pp (95% CI +1.36 to +3.25) on a corpus it was never tuned for. A Lieder-only fine-tune improving general-music recognition is not something we assumed would happen, and it is the strongest single result here. 448 gives back half of it.

Why a third benchmark

OSSQ is independent but small and stylistically narrow. PDMX is a large public corpus whose validation split predates all of this, so it tests whether the OSSQ regression is a property of the model or of one repertoire. Its result is pending and will be added here as measured, whichever way it falls.

One caveat is already known and stated up front: replay sampling for these runs drew from an index that contained the PDMX validation rows, so roughly 3.6% of that benchmark is contaminated. It inflates all three checkpoints equally, so the comparison holds while the absolute figure does not. The bug is fixed; no clean absolute PDMX number will be quoted until a checkpoint trains without it.

How the corpus is built, and why it can disagree with itself

The training pairs are staff-image crops with symbolic labels. Producing them means deciding which measures of a digital transcription correspond to which crop of a scanned page — and getting that wrong produces labels that look perfectly well-formed while describing different music from the image.

So the alignment is computed twice, by methods that share no evidence:

MethodEvidence usedFailure mode
Bar-count alignment Physical barlines detected in the image, matched to transcription measure counts by dynamic programming Blind to content — a range with the right number of bars is accepted whatever is in them
Reverse fingerprinting The model's own reading of each crop, segmented against the transcription Model-derived, so it agrees with the model by construction

Neither is trustworthy alone, and the second is circular for evaluation purposes. The rule that follows is the load-bearing idea of the whole rebuild:

Where the two methods agree, the label is admissible as evaluation data. Where they disagree, it may still be used for training — but never to judge the model.

That yields 3,964 evaluation-admissible pairs and 8,175 training pairs. Filtering an evaluation set by agreement with the model would measure the model against itself; this is why the reverse method is firewalled out of the evaluation path even though it is the more accurate of the two.

A parser bug was hiding a quarter of the corpus

The MusicXML parser raised on <octave-shift> (86 scores) and <clef-octave-change> (11), discarding those scores whole — for a routine piano engraving mark. Handling both recovers 94 of 111 scores: 3,214 → 4,587 clean pairs, 165 → 234 scores.

The subtlety is that MusicXML <pitch> is the sounding pitch while the label must carry the written one, so the correction is written − sounding — and 8va is exported as type="down". Only ~1% of notes are affected, which means no aggregate metric can see the sign: running it both ways gave byte-identical corpus statistics. Human review settled it at 22/25 octaves correct with zero octave errors.

What human review overturned

Four automated judgements were checked against a human reviewer. All four were wrong, and all four were wrong in the same direction — discarding good data. That consistency is itself the finding: an automated pipeline that errs conservatively looks healthy from the inside, because the evidence it would need to notice is precisely what it discarded.

  1. Rejecting on disagreement was wrong 32 times out of 33. Disagreement means one method is right, not that both are wrong — bar-count correct 28, content 5, neither 1. No score threshold separates the cases, so the rule became: prefer the bar-count label, mark it arbitrated, keep it out of evaluation.
  2. "No content found" actually meant "no pitched notes". The fingerprinter dropped rests, so a rest-heavy system produced no tokens, scored 0.0 for every candidate span, and lost to the empty move. Reviewed at 30 of 30 wrong. This single bug produced most of the disagreements in the corpus: fixing it moved 822 arbitrated → 258 and 3,376 consensus → 3,968, and of 45 pairs the reviewer flagged as wrongly rejected, 40 became plain consensus and none stayed discarded.
  3. Stratifying the split by score alone left validation unable to see the defect. All 113 many-to-many and reference-line-split pairs landed in train, making validation 100% one-to-one — structurally incapable of detecting the exact problem the rebuild exists to remove. Strata must be derived from the manifest being split, per rare topology.
  4. The review generator silently produced two empty review sets after the verdict vocabulary changed under it. Same class of quiet failure as an under-covered corpus: no error, no output, no alarm.

The blind spot we can name

Consensus cannot detect the case where both methods agree and both are wrong. This is confirmed, not hypothetical.

IMSLP183806-sys1-v1 is a densely written piano grand staff. Its bar-count label reads R R | R R | R R — three measures of pure rests. Its content label has notes, but five measures for a three-measure crop. The reviewer judged neither correct. The pipeline classifies the pair consensus, which would admit it to the evaluation set.

The mechanism is specific and worth stating precisely: consensus is decided from the voice-0 reading and applied to every staff in the system, so a system-level agreement does not validate each voice's label. Nothing currently checks per-voice content. That is the next thing to build, and until it exists the evaluation set carries an unmeasured error floor of this kind.

Open problem: a discard rule that measures the wrong thing

An implied tuplet is engraved with no bracket and no numeral — the performer infers it. Neither the transcription nor the model records one, so the bar sums to more than its metre holds. Training on it teaches the model to write overfull bars, so the builder discards any pair containing such a bar: 417 pairs across 144 scores, roughly 10% of the clean corpus.

Those pairs had never been written to disk, so nobody had looked at one. Quarantining rather than deleting them made two things visible, and both say the rule is not doing what its name claims.

First: most of them are not tuplets

Classifying the 546 offending bars by how much they overflow separates causes that the single rule had been treating alike:

161

tuplet-shaped — excess is exactly what an unbracketed tuplet adds

80

exactly 2× or 3× the bar — a barline nobody placed

76

a second metre — the crop changes time signature

229

unexplained by any of the above

So the rule is doing two opposite jobs at once. On the tuplet bars it discards good music over a notation the label cannot express. On the integer-multiple bars it is correctly rejecting a misaligned label, and recovering those would poison the corpus. "Recover the 417" would have been the wrong instruction, and a single review set asking whether to keep them would have averaged the two into an unanswerable question.

Second, and worse: the test is unsound for 89% of them

371 of the 417 discarded pairs are grand staves. The corpus that is kept is 44.9% grand staff. Put as a rate:

Discard rate by staff type
Staff typeKeptDiscardedDiscard rate
Single staff2,299462.0%
Grand staff1,87337116.5%

A grand staff is discarded at 8.4 times the rate of a single staff. Implied tuplets are not distributed that way. The mechanism is already documented elsewhere in this codebase: group_into_chords takes the minimum duration across a chord, so on a grand staff a bar where the hands play different rhythms is neither their sum nor either hand's own length. The corpus audit refuses to run any duration-dependent check on a grand staff for exactly this reason — and the discard rule calls the same arithmetic with no such guard.

The result is a comparison of two equally distorted numbers. Bars where the hands happen to align keep their true length while the modal bar is pulled short by the ones that do not, so the aligned bars read as overfull. That is a plausible mechanism for a pure artefact, and the 8.4× enrichment is what it would look like.

Stated, not fixed

This is not being silently corrected. The evidence says the rule is unsound on grand staves, but an unknown fraction of those 371 pairs have real problems that the broken test happened to catch, and the corpus cannot tell the two apart. Two review sets are built instead — 46 single-staff pairs at complete coverage where the arithmetic holds and the tuplet question is answerable, and a sample of the 371 grand staves asking only whether the label matches the scan. What comes back decides the rule.

The general shape is the same one that produced the checkpoint result at the top of this page: a measurement that was never valid, reported for long enough to become a fact. In both cases the number looked reasonable, moved in a plausible direction, and nothing in the pipeline was in a position to contradict it.

Reproducing any number on this page

Each checkpoint is scored to a .jsonl of per-staff reference and prediction vectors, then compared pairwise:

# score one checkpoint on one benchmark
python -m training.transformer.base_predictions \
  --index datasets/pdmx/index_valid.txt \
  --checkpoint <path> --out pdmx_new.jsonl

# paired comparison, bootstrap interval, win/loss split
python -m training.transformer.compare_checkpoints \
  --name "OSSQ" \
  --run 447=general_mid.jsonl \
  --run 448=general_new.jsonl

Three properties are deliberate. Reference and prediction are padded to a common width, so a free-running model that diverges in length is penalised rather than scored on the overlap it happened to get right. Only staves scored by every run are compared, so a checkpoint that failed on some crop is not credited with an easier subset. And both overall (positions pooled) and macro (branches averaged) are printed, because they answer different questions and are allowed to disagree in public.

Checkpoints 426 (base), 447, 448. Lieder and OSSQ figures measured 2026-08-28 on 792 and 292 staves respectively; PDMX pending. Bootstrap intervals are 2,000 resamples over staves, seed 0.

447 is the current best checkpoint. 448 is not being shipped, and the run that produced it is being ablated to find out which change caused the regression.
